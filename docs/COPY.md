- What is Chiari malformation?
- What it is
- The lowest part of the cerebellum, the tonsils, extends below the foramen magnum, the opening at the base of the skull. The cerebellum helps control balance and coordination. It's often linked to a lower back part of the skull that is smaller or shaped differently than usual, which crowds the brain. Less often, it develops later in life for other reasons.
- Who has it
- Most people are born with the skull differences that lead to it. Some never have symptoms; others first notice them as teens or adults.
- How it's found
- Usually with an MRI scan and a doctor's exam, sometimes by chance during a scan for something else. A neurologist or neurosurgeon looks at the scan together with your symptoms.
- What it can feel like
- Chiari looks different for everyone, and many people with Type I have no symptoms at all.
- The classic sign is a headache at the back of the head that comes on after coughing, sneezing or straining.
- Other symptoms people report
- Neck pain
- Dizziness and trouble with balance
- Numbness or tingling in the hands and feet
- Weakness or trouble with fine hand movements
- Trouble swallowing, or voice changes
- Breathing or sleep problems, such as sleep apnea
- Ringing in the ears
- Blurry or double vision
- A curve in the spine (scoliosis), especially in children
<!-- 2026-11-04 accuracy audit (text items 5, 7): was "Less often: blurry or shaky vision"; scoliosis added. Sources: Cleveland Clinic, NINDS, MedlinePlus. -->
- Common misunderstandings
- "It's just a bad headache."
- Chiari is a structural difference that can affect balance, nerves and the flow of spinal fluid, not only headaches.
- "The measurement tells the whole story."
- Doctors traditionally use about 5 mm below the opening as a cutoff. How far the tonsils extend doesn't always match how someone feels. Some people with a clear descent have no symptoms, and some with less than 5 mm do. Doctors also look at the shape of the tonsils, spinal fluid flow, and whether a syrinx is present.

- Qué es
- Las amígdalas cerebelosas, la parte más baja del cerebelo, se extienden por debajo de la abertura en la base del cráneo. Suele estar relacionada con que la parte posterior e inferior del cráneo es más pequeña o tiene otra forma, lo que deja poco espacio al cerebro. - Con menos frecuencia: visión borrosa o temblorosa
- Muchas personas con Chiari tipo I no tienen ningún síntoma.
- Ideas equivocadas comunes
- "Es solo un dolor de cabeza fuerte".
- También puede afectar el equilibrio, los nervios y el flujo del líquido cefalorraquídeo.

- Typical anatomy
- The tonsils usually sit at or above the dashed line across the skull opening (a few millimeters below can still be normal), and spinal fluid moves freely around them.
- Chiari I
- The tonsils extend below the opening, at least about 5 mm by the usual definition. In one study of 160 people with Chiari I, the average was about 12 mm, but it ranged from 5 to 25 mm, and the number alone doesn't decide who needs surgery. They are often pointed rather than rounded. The fluid space behind them is often crowded, and in many people fluid flow slows or is blocked there.

- Chiari I with syrinx
- A syrinx is a fluid-filled cavity inside the spinal cord. It often develops alongside Chiari, most often in the neck part of the spinal cord.
- After decompression
- Surgeons remove a small piece of bone at the back of the skull opening and usually the back of the first vertebra. Often they also open and patch the brain's covering to make more room for spinal fluid. The tonsils often rise only partly. Surgery improves or stabilizes symptoms in most people, though damage that has already happened may not fully reverse, and some people need more than one operation. Results vary, and surgery isn't right for everyone.
- Cerebrum
- The largest part of the brain, responsible for thinking, movement and the senses.
- Corpus callosum
- A thick band of nerve fibers connecting the left and right halves of the brain.
- Cerebellum
- The back part of the brain that helps control balance and coordination.
- Fourth ventricle
- A small fluid-filled space between the brainstem and cerebellum, where spinal fluid passes through.
- Brainstem
- Connects the brain to the spinal cord and runs automatic functions like breathing, heart rate and swallowing.
- Cerebellar tonsil
- The two lowest parts of the cerebellum. In Chiari, they extend below the opening at the base of the skull and are often pointed (peg-shaped) rather than rounded. They really sit just to either side of the midline and often descend unevenly; the model shows them at their lowest point, where MRI measures them.
- Foramen magnum
- The opening at the base of the skull where the brainstem becomes the spinal cord.
- Spinal cord
- Carries signals between the brain and the rest of the body.
- Spinal fluid (CSF)
- Cerebrospinal fluid: clear fluid that cushions the brain and spinal cord. With each heartbeat it pulses back and forth between the skull and the spinal canal.
- C2 vertebra
- The second bone of the neck. Its upward peg, the dens, sits just in front of the spinal cord.
