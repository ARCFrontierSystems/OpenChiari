 1. Introduction
 Hans Chiari first described cerebellar tonsillar herniation (TH) through the foramen magnum (FM) on postmortem specimens, a finding that came to be known as Chiari malformation type 1 (CM1). Diagnosis of CM1 was later based on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scanning showing cerebellar tonsillar displacement at least 5 mm below the FM [ 1 ]. Chiari’s original cases had associated hydrocephalus. Occipital bone hypoplasia and small posterior cranial fossa (SPCF) are other CM1-associated conditions detected in imaging studies [ 2 , 3 ].
 Investigators proposed that a mechanism of CM1 pathogenesis was a disproportion between the size of the posterior fossa and its neural contents. TH resulted because the entire hindbrain volume was too large to fit in an underdeveloped, low-volume SPCF [ 4 , 5 ]. CM1 associated with SPCF was called primary or “classic” CM1. On the other hand, TH, accompanied by increased intracranial pressure, decreased intraspinal pressure, or other causes but without SPCF, was called secondary CM1 [ 6 ]. TH into cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) pathways can obstruct CSF pathways around the FM with and without PCF structural dysmorphism [ 6 – 8 ].
 CM1 symptoms arise from CSF flow obstruction and compression of the brainstem, cerebellum, or cranial nerves in the FM [ 9 ]. TH in mm is indirectly related to clinical symptoms because more significant TH generally reduces CSF flow and increases neural compression [ 10 , 11 ]. However, some patients with normal tonsillar position (<3 mm) and SPCF can have prominent CM1-like clinical manifestations, including syringomyelia ( Fig. 1 ) [ 12 , 13 ]. Investigators coined Chiari type 0 malformation to describe patients with a normal tonsillar position and syringomyelia whose syringomyelia resolves after PCF decompression [ 14 , 15 ]. Another term, CM1.5, was created to describe patients with ≥5 mm caudal displacement of the brainstem and TH. CM1.5 is often associated with syringomyelia but not spina bifida [ 16 ].

 Recognizing the various subtypes of CM1 has clinical relevance [ 17 ]. Studies of CM1 twins and CM1 families show that symptomatic patients with CM0 and CM1 have inherited SPCF [ 18 , 19 ]. The subtypes of classic CM1 (CM0, CM0.5, and CM1.5) are related through SPCF and form a continuum. Morphological findings such as the extent of caudal hindbrain displacement, neural element crowding within the PCF and FM, and the presence or absence of a syrinx can be used for CM1 subtyping [ 20 – 23 ]. However, the CM1 spectrum abnormalities have controversial definitions, classification systems, and reliability of radiological assessment [ 20 – 23 ]. TH alone often does not predict which patients will have CM1 symptoms or syringomyelia. A new CM subtyping system was introduced after studying morphometric parameters in 141 CM1, 24 CM1.5, and 7 CM0 patients [ 11 ]. This system diagnosed classic CM1 and CM1.5 patients with 90% accuracy and CM0 patients with 50–71% accuracy [ 11 ]. Recently, Heffez and colleagues reported on surgically treated patients with CM1-clinical symptoms, medullary and cerebellar tonsillar compression, TH < 3 mm, and absent syringomyelia [ 10 ]. MRI verified neural tissue distortion preoperatively.
 Imprecise diagnostic criteria for the CM1 subtypes may lead to incorrect diagnoses and unnecessary surgical procedures. CM1 subtype diagnosis should depend on radiological and clinical markers that predict symptomatic conditions that respond to surgical intervention. Thus, careful consideration of the distinctiveness of the CM0 entity and elaboration of the radiological diagnostic criteria are needed. This chapter reviews CM1, the CM1 subtypes, CM0, borderline CM1, and CM1.5, and current thinking about hindbrain herniation and the SPCF.
 2. The Cerebellar Tonsils Position and Diagnostics of CM1 Spectrum Disorders
 The MRI finding of ≥5 mm of cerebellar TH below the FM was adopted as the diagnostic criteria for CM1 because it predicted CM1 symptoms with 100% sensitivity and 98.5% specificity. The lowest normal tonsillar position was considered 3 mm of TH, with a sensitivity of 96% and specificity of 99.5% [ 1 ]. Later an age-dependent CM1 threshold of 6 mm in patients up to 10 years, 5 mm in 10–30 years, 4 mm in 30–70 years, and 3 mm for 70 years was demonstrated [ 24 , 25 ]. Smith and colleagues examined 2400 randomly selected patients from a database undergoing MRI and showed that asymptomatic persons’ lowest cerebellar tonsillar position relative to the FM followed a normal distribution [ 25 ]. In this study, 1–2% of asymptomatic subjects had TH > 5 mm. Using a TH ≥ 5 mm diagnostic threshold would, therefore, make a CM1 diagnosis in 1–2% of asymptomatic persons [ 25 ]. Other criteria like PCF crowdedness and medullary descent may reduce these 1–2% false positive CM1 diagnoses and improve the specificity of MRI to diagnose symptomatic CM1 [ 11 , 26 ]. Patients with CM1 or borderline CM1, characterized by CM1-like symptoms and 3–5 mm TH, may have hindbrain overcrowding [ 4 , 10 , 12 – 14 , 27 ]. Patients with normal tonsillar position, cervical syringomyelia, and syrinx resolution after PFD receive the diagnosis of “Chiari zero” malformation (CM0) or “tight cisterna magna” [ 15 , 20 , 28 ]. However, patients with CM1-like symptoms and signs but without syringomyelia are diagnosed with “CM-like syndrome,” CM1 spectrum disorder,” “FM syndrome,” or “CM0-only” [ 13 , 20 , 27 , 29 ].
 A recently published international consensus document defines CM1 as a “paraxial mesoderm disorder, with abnormalities of the posterior cranial fossa (mostly small) and the consequent herniation of the cerebellar tonsils. CM1-A: with syringomyelia on MRI; CM1-B: without syringomyelia on MRI” [ 30 ]. This CM1 definition includes a developmental PCF abnormality but not a minimum TH diagnostic threshold. This CM1 definition allows patients with SPCF and any amount of TH below the FM to be diagnosed with CM1. This CM1 definition separates TH of any amount associated with congenitally small PCF (also named “true,” primary,” or “classical” CM1) from nonmalformative, acquired TH, which was previously called secondary CM1 or acquired tonsillar ectopia [ 6 , 30 ].
 3. Epidemiology of TH/CM1 and Small Posterior Cranial Fossa (SPCF) Heritability
 In 0.8–0.9% of normal adults and 1.0–3.6% of children undergoing MRI, TH was 5 mm or more below the FM [ 31 , 32 ]. Clusters of a significantly higher prevalence of CM1 were reported in geographical regions and ethnic groups [ 33 , 34 ]. The estimated prevalence of symptomatic CM1 in Tatarstan and the United States is about 1:2500–1:10,000 [ 34 ]. In northwestern Italy, the prevalence of symptomatic CM1 is 7.74:100,000 [ 35 ]. An extensive nationwide healthcare network in the United States documented significantly increased rates of CM1 decompression surgery in children and adults since 2000. The actual prevalence of CM0 is unknown. In a cohort of patients with CM1 signs and symptoms, 97 patients had MRI findings of TH 0–<3 mm, 148 patients with TH 3–5 mm, and 183 with MRI-verified CM1 with TH > 5 mm [ 10 ]. In surgical series of patients with CM1 symptoms, 4–9% had CM0 [ 17 , 36 , 37 ]. The prevalence of SPCF traits like short clivus length and tight cisterna magna is 8% in normal adult individuals in the United States [ 38 ]. Epidemiologic studies and reviews suggest that CM1 is not rare ( Fig. 2 ) [ 39 – 41 ].

 Tonsil position in normal persons is distributed like other normally distributed morphometric features of multifactorial inheritance like height and head circumference [ 25 ]. In a surgical series, 12% of symptomatic patients with classical CM1 had a positive family history of CM1 [ 4 ]. Genetic and external factors can predispose to CM1 development and mesodermal abnormalities [ 42 , 43 ]. Some PCF traits in CM1-affected families are heritable, and more than half were associated with disease status [ 43 ]. PCF volume, basal angulation, and height are highly heritable, while TH extent is not [ 44 , 45 ]. Patients with SPCF may later develop TH from environmental influences [ 43 , 44 ]. Studies of monozygotic twins, triplets, and families affected by CM1 demonstrate the inheritance of SPCF with variable TH expression [ 18 , 19 , 46 ]. There is increasing evidence of the complex genetic basis of PCF development and the genetic heterogeneity of classical CM1 with many gene candidates [ 45 – 48 ].
 4. Imaging of CM1 Spectrum Disorders
 The clinical symptoms of CM1 itself and associated syringomyelia are often nonspecific [ 4 , 13 , 49 ]. CM1 is usually diagnosed using the morphometric measurement of TH ≥ 5 mm. However, measuring the tonsillar position on MRI scans is inexact. In one study, seven operators measured the tonsillar position in CM1 patients and controls. The authors found such high variability among operators that they recommended confirmation of TH measurements by two radiologists [ 50 ]. However, persons with an MRI diagnosis of CM1 confirmed by TH ≥ 5 mm may be asymptomatic [ 51 , 52 ]. Neural compression and CSF pathway obstruction at the FM elicit CM1 symptoms [ 9 ]. CM1 patients with TH > 12–14 mm are usually symptomatic [ 51 ].
 Investigators are studying ways to improve the MRI measurement of TH. One group recommended using coronal rather than sagittal images to measure right and left TH and diagnose CM1 [ 53 ]. Cough headache correlated better with the amount of parasagittal TH than midsagittal TH [ 54 ]. Symptomatic CM1 pediatric patients may have different amounts of left and right TH. The tonsillar asymmetry corresponds to hindbrain herniation asymmetry in 18% of patients [ 55 ]. In adult CM1 patients with syringomyelia, most (91%) tonsillar herniations were asymmetrical. Asymmetrical syringes are often off-center to the side of the more caudal tonsil [ 56 ]. Twenty-three percent of CM1 patients with an asymmetrical floor of the PCF have asymmetrical tonsillar ectopia [ 57 ]. Cerebellar peg-like rather than rounded tonsils are molded into that shape in the FM and are more common in patients with TH > 5 mm [ 25 ].
 The extent of cerebellar TH may vary over time, decreasing (31–38%), increasing (4–12%), or staying stable (50–65%) [ 24 ]. Studies of age-related changes in normal tonsillar position show a trend toward a more cranial tonsil position with advancing age [ 24 , 25 ]. Early in life, the caudal tonsillar displacement decreases; in middle age, it increases and stabilizes for an extended period; and in advancing age, it steadily decreases again [ 25 ]. An MRI study comparing CM1 morphology in patients of different genders showed that male CMI patients had more prominent morphometric abnormalities despite the higher female prevalence of CM1 [ 26 ].
 CM0-and CM1-related syringomyelia can resolve after a spontaneous reduction in TH or other process enlarging CSF flow pathways at the FM ( Fig. 3 ) [ 59 – 61 ]. Conversely, increasing TH can initiate symptomatic CM1 [ 62 ]. The PCF crowdedness index declines with age [ 63 ]. Small changes in TH may reduce or increase symptoms in CM1, borderline CM1, and CM0 cases. Head movement and position can change the amount of hindbrain and cerebellar TH in patients with CM1 and SPCF. In most patients with CM1, the cerebellar tonsils descend (mean 1.4 mm) in cervical flexion and ascend (mean 1.2 mm) in cervical extension [ 53 ]. In an MRI study, patients with limited tonsillar ectopia while supine had increased TH when upright [ 64 ].

 4.1. The Small and Overcrowded Posterior Cranial Fossa. Imaging Approaches Assessing SPCF
 Five years after initially describing TH and analyzing another case, Hans Chiari proposed a mechanism for TH development, insufficient bone growth, and skull enlargement [ 65 ]. Insufficient growth of the para-axial mesoderm and occipital somites [ 66 ] leads to reduced development of the basichondrocranium ranging from isolated hypoplasia of parts of the occipital bone to SPCF. The underdeveloped PCF is small and flattened, resulting in a mismatch between the PCF’s and neural contents’ configurations. A contemporary theory proposes that the mechanism of classical CM1 formation is overcrowding of the PCF, causing the descent of the cerebellar tonsils and hindbrain [ 4 – 6 , 23 , 27 , 57 , 67 ]. This theory of SPCF as the cause of CM1 has many advocates [ 30 , 40 ] and is supported by studies of large cohorts of patients [ 23 , 68 , 69 ]. Sometimes, the hindbrain volume disproportion to hypoplastic PCF volume is plain and verifiable by simple radiographic measurements. In other cases, this disproportion is confirmed only after analyzing CT and MRI measurements [ 6 , 23 , 57 ]. Mechanisms other than crowdedness may contribute to the development of classical CM1 [ 70 – 72 ]. Posterior fossa hypoplasia is sometimes accompanied by basilar invagination, atlantal assimilation, oropharyngeal anomaly, and congenital cervical canal stenosis [ 4 , 48 , 73 ].
 In studies elaborating diagnostic criteria, elucidating pathophysiology, evaluating methods of treatment, and assessing treatment efficacy, the following morphometric approaches separately or in combination have been used: (a) measurements of the occipital bone components (basiocciput, exocciput, and supraocciput) and quantification of PCF crowdedness; (b) measurement of the PCF volume; (c) assessment of PCF configuration by angulometric measurements reflecting the type and grade of PCF flattening; (d) measurement of distances from hindbrain and cerebellar landmarks to the FM reflecting caudal position; (e) characterization of morphometric features of the FM-C1 funnel, crowdedness of CSF spaces, and the size and deformity of the medulla and cerebellar tonsils [ 4 – 7 , 11 , 13 , 23 , 26 , 27 , 70 – 72 , 74 ]. The first three approaches (a–c) can be used to estimate for the severity of SPCF, the cause of hindbrain descent, and TH; the fourth (d) reflects the consequences of the poor fit between the PCF osseous container and its contents; and the fifth (e) concerns factors like neural crowdedness and abnormal FM size and shape that can encourage caudal shift of the neural elements, mold and compress the cerebellar tonsils, and narrow the CSF spaces.
 4.2. Measurements of the PCF in CM1 Spectrum Disorders
 Many studies have demonstrated SPCF in CM1, CM0, and craniosynostosis [ 2 , 3 , 18 ]. Most studies showed that the basiocciput, clivus, and supraocciput, alone or in combination, were significantly shortened in CM1 patients [ 5 , 7 , 11 , 57 , 67 , 75 – 78 ]. The occipital condyle (exocciput) was small in another CM1 study [ 6 ]. Other studies showed that a single linear measure did not reliably predict PCF volume [ 8 , 79 ]. The PCF of CM0 patients resembles that of CM1 patients [ 12 , 13 , 27 , 37 , 77 ]. In CM0 and CM1, similar abnormalities are seen in eight linear morphometric measures of short bones and flattening of PCF and two measures of hindbrain caudal shifting [ 27 ]. CM0 and CM1 patients have abnormal CSF flow at the CCJ [ 80 ].
 MRI evaluation of CM0 patients without syringomyelia should include evaluation of SPCF morphometric markers and tonsillar ectopia. In one study, an abnormally short clivus distinguished symptomatic CM1-borderline and CM0 patients (TH > 3.9 ± 1.0 mm) from adult asymptomatic individuals with low-lying tonsils (TH > 1.5 ± 1.1 mm) [ 77 ]. This finding highlights the association of clivus shortening with CM1 spectrum disorder symptomatology and pathophysiology. Clivus shortening is also associated with the cephalad location of the odontoid tip and basilar invagination [ 81 ]. Angular measurements show that CM1 spectrum patients have horizontalization of the clivus, reduced PCF height, and flattening of the PCF [ 7 , 12 , 27 , 82 – 84 ]. Reduced PCF height reduces the distances between the pons, fastigium, corpus callosum, and the FM and promotes hindbrain caudal descent [ 7 , 11 ]. A horizontal clivus and reduced PCF height are also seen in CM1 patients with syringomyelia [ 85 , 86 ]. The FM’s size and shape also affect hindbrain and cerebellar tonsillar caudal positioning [ 87 ]. Thus, the pathogenesis of CM0 and CM1 depends on abnormalities in the PCF’s configuration, volume, and size and the FM’s size and shape.
 In patients with CM1, morphometric abnormalities occur in various regions of the PCF [ 23 , 42 , 43 ]. Raybaud and Jallo [ 88 ] characterized the CM1 PCF abnormalities: (a) a proportionately small basiocciput (clivus) type with flattening of the PCF; (b) a proportionately small supraocciput type; and (c) a type with globally small occipital bones, with shortness of both the clival and the supraoccipital segments. Variations of SPCF were also classified by Batzdorf and colleagues: (a) anterior type: encroachment on the posterior fossa: basilar invagination, platybasia, odontoid invagination; (b) roof type: low insertion of the tentorium cerebelli into the occipital bone, a low-lying torcula, an almost vertical tentorium paralleling the clivus on sagittal imaging; (c) floor type: thickened occipital bone, horizontal occipital bone, upward sloping posterior edge of foramen magnum [ 89 , 90 ]. Urbizu and colleagues confirmed the phenotypic heterogeneity of classic CM1 [ 48 ].
 4.3. PCF Morphometrics in CM1 Spectrum Disorders
 PCF volume analysis has improved [ 67 ]. Studies using manual, automated, and semiautomated measurement techniques showed that CM1 patients have smaller PCF volumes and smaller PCF to intracranial volume ratios than normal controls [ 4 , 6 , 57 , 89 , 91 ]. The hindbrain volume: PCF volume ratio estimates PCF crowdedness [ 63 ] and is significantly higher in CM1 patients than in healthy controls [ 5 ]. Most patients with classical CM1 have reduced PCF volume or other PCF morphologic abnormalities associated with hindbrain descent [ 4 , 5 ]. Patients with classical CM1 can have reduced PCF volume broadly or only around the FM. Patients with CM1-borderline and TH < 5 mm have reduced volume only around the FM [ 23 ]. In another study, CM1 patients with and without basilar invagination had PCF volumes that were significantly smaller than controls [ 91 ].
 CM1 children with syringomyelia have significantly smaller-than-normal PCF volumes [ 85 ]. In our study of adult classical CM1 and CM0 patients with and without syringomyelia, patients with syringomyelia had significantly smaller PCF volumes than controls [ 27 ]. In this study, the PCF midsagittal areas of patients with and without syringomyelia were significantly less than control [ 27 ]. Other studies measuring the PCF midsagittal areas of CM1 patients reported smaller than normal PCF areas [ 2 , 7 , 26 , 78 , 84 , 92 ]. Biswas and colleagues reported that CM1 patients had decreased PCF area anterior to the brain stem, which accounted for their smaller midsagittal PCF area compared to controls [ 26 ]. Treatment outcomes after craniocervical decompression are improved when bone removal significantly increases PCF volume [ 67 ].
 Many studies report SPCF in CM1 patients with severe symptoms or syringomyelia. However, other studies report similar PCF volumes in CM1 patients and controls [ 8 , 70 , 93 , 94 ]. Some CM1 patients have reduced volume around the FM but normal PCF volume. Nishikawa’s new classification system includes this type of CM1 patient [ 23 , 68 , 69 ]. His system was developed by examining 500 patients with CM1 spectrum disorders. It built on earlier research into the pathogenesis of hindbrain (brainstem and cerebellum) ptosis [ 6 , 23 ]. He described the following CM1 subtypes: (1) CM1 type A (corresponding to “acquired tonsillar ectopia” [ 30 ]) is unassociated with basichondrocranium hypoplasia or PCF deformity and is caused by craniocervical junction (CCJ) instability, hydrocephalus, intracranial mass lesions, spinal CSF leak, and tethered cord syndrome. Almost 40% of CM1 type A patients had CCJ instability [ 68 ]; (2) CM1 type B (TH > 5 mm) is characterized by small occipital bone size, small volume around the FM (VAFM), and normal volume of the PCF (VPCF). The VPCF stays normal because reduced volume around the FM is offset by the volume gained superiorly under an abnormally steep tentorium; (3) CM1 type C (TH > 5 mm) is characterized by diminished VPCF, VAFM, reduced occipital bone size, and an elongated and caudally displaced brain stem. Hindbrain ptosis results from underdevelopment of the occipital bone, a small VAFM, and an increased VBPCF/VPCF ratio (the volume of the brain in the PCF (VBPCF)/the volume of the PCF). The normal volume hindbrain is crowded into the shallow PCF with underdeveloped occipital bone, leading to caudal ptosis of the brainstem and cerebellum; (4) CM1 with TH < 5 mm (CM-borderline and CM0) is characterized by a small VAFM and normal PCF volume. Crowdedness of the hindbrain in the FM in these patients arises from occipital bone underdevelopment and focal shallowness of the area around the FM. Among patients of the CM-borderline subtype, 10% had CCJ instability, while among CM1 subtypes B and C, 1–4% had CCJ instability [ 68 ]. In the CM-borderline, CM0, CM1 B, and CM1 C subtypes, neurological symptoms and CSF flow obstruction result from compression of the hindbrain and CSF cisterns in the FM [ 4 , 27 , 28 , 67 , 80 ]. CM1 morphometric studies that include patients with CM1 subtypes with normal PCF volumes and focal shallowness around the FM and “CM1 type A” (acquired tonsillar ectopia with normal PCF volumes) are biased toward normal values. The inclusion of these subgroups could explain why some studies report normal PCF volume in CM1 patients.
 4.4. The Foramen Magnum Area: Morphometric Features of the FM-C1 Funnel, CSF Spaces, Neural Crowdedness, and Deformity of the Medulla and Cerebellar Tonsils
 The occipital bone’s hypoplasia leads to decreased PCF volume and crowdedness in the foramen magnum and FM-C1 funnel [ 9 ]. The pathophysiology of CM1 symptoms depends on the FM’s size, configuration, and crowdedness [ 74 , 87 , 95 ]. In CM1, developmental bony and soft tissue anomalies at the craniovertebral junction narrow the intradural space. A cross-sectional MRI assessment in the FM plane evaluates the hindbrain tissue compression or distortion characteristic of the CM1 subtypes. Some studies reported that CM1 patients have increased anteroposterior FM diameter on midsagittal MRI, while others reported normal or short anteroposterior FM diameters [ 82 , 83 , 96 ]. However, the anteroposterior length of the FM, brainstem, and cerebellum did not differ between CM1 patients and controls in a meta-analysis of data from 12 studies [ 2 ]. Yuksel reported that the McRae line was significantly longer in CM1 patients with TH > 10 mm than in patients with TH < 10 mm and suggested that McRae line elongation and symptom severity are predictive markers in decision-making for surgical intervention [ 97 ].
 The cross-sectional area of the FM is more helpful than linear measurements of the FM anteroposterior diameter in assessing FM stenosis in CM1 patients. An anatomical study showed that skulls with larger intracranial volumes had larger FM areas [ 98 ]. In contrast, patients with syndromic craniosynostosis had abnormally small FM areas [ 6 ]. The FM area was significantly enlarged in patients with TH and tethered cord syndrome but without SPCF [ 6 , 99 ]. The calculated FM area was smaller in adult patients with classic CM1 and CM0, and SPCF MRI traits than in controls [ 19 ]. In the largest group of classic CM1 patients, detailed FM CT measurements showed that the FM anterior-posterior diameter in CM1 patients did not differ from controls. In contrast, FM’s transverse diameter and inferior and superior outlet area were significantly smaller [ 2 ]. In our MRI study of several classical CM1 subtypes, all groups trended toward reduced FM area. However, statistical significance was seen only in the CM0 group with SPCF, without TH, and with syringomyelia [ 27 ]. In CM1, reduced FM area accentuates FM crowdedness and could restrict TH. Aydin et al. hypothesized that downward herniation of the cerebellar tonsils in utero might adaptively broaden the anteroposterior FM diameter [ 75 ]. However, it is unclear in CM1 if the pressure of TH expands the FM or if a congenitally expanded FM encourages TH.
 4.5. The Morphometric Markers of a Small and Crowded PCF in Classical CM1 Spectrum Disorders
 The features and severity of CM1 symptoms relate to neural and CSF pathway crowdedness within the FM funnel [ 10 , 74 , 95 ]. Occipital hypoplasia reduces the PCF height, the volume around the FM, or the volume of the PCF more broadly. If in classic CM1 patients, short bones, SPCF, and deformed PCF shape are the anatomic factors responsible for hindbrain descent and foramen of Magendie compression, measurements of the underdevelopment of osseous structures should correlate with measures of hindbrain caudal position. Literature is sparse on this topic.
 Traditionally, 5 mm of tonsillar descent below the FM on MRI is the diagnostic marker of CM1 [ 1 ]. However, asymptomatic persons receive false-positive diagnoses of CM1 using this TH threshold. Symptomatic patients with borderline CM1 receive (false) negative diagnoses ruling out CM1 [ 10 , 11 , 67 ]. This diagnostic problem arises because caudal hindbrain displacement and PCF underdevelopment in classic CM1 alters several radiographic measurements in varying degrees, not only TH. In classic CM1 entities, the caudal hindbrain position shortens the distances between different neural structures and the FM [ 7 , 11 , 37 , 78 ]. In CM1 patients, the distance from the obex to the FM was more strongly associated with syringomyelia, scoliosis, and PFD surgery than TH [ 101 ]. In CM0, the obex may have a caudal position even though tonsillar ectopia is minimal [ 15 , 27 ]. In CM1.5, caudal obex position accompanies significant TH and a flattened medulla, but not caudal displacement of the pons or midbrain [ 16 ]. In pediatric CM1, increased tonsillar ectopia was associated with an inferiorly displaced obex, widened FM, and posterior odontoid angulation [ 102 ]. Some, but not all studies showed that the PCF volume was inversely correlated with TH [ 57 , 67 , 89 ].
 The classic CM1 and CM1 subtypes have a caudal hindbrain position, short clivus, and reduced distances between the fastigium, pons, corpus callosum, and FM. Classic CM1 and CM1 subtypes often have flattening of the PCF reflected by altered basal, clivocanal, and Boogaard’s angles [ 11 , 82 ]. Symptomatic CM0 and CM1-borderline patients have anatomic restrictions to caudal movement of the cerebellar tonsils at the FM that contribute to neural tissue and CSF space compression [ 10 , 23 , 27 ].
 5. Symptomatic and Asymptomatic Classic CM1 Spectrum Disorders
 Patients with TH of ≥5 mm may be asymptomatic [ 25 , 51 , 52 ]. CM1 morphometric studies compared MRI measures in symptomatic CM1 patients to control groups without TH [ 2 , 3 ]. Few studies compare symptomatic and asymptomatic patients with TH. In a study of pediatric CM1 patients, the mean PCF volume was smaller in CM1 patients than in control patients. The mean PCF volume/intracranial volume proportions for asymptomatic and symptomatic CM1 patients were similar for children aged 0–9 years but different for children aged 10–18. Another group found no significant differences between pediatric patients with symptomatic and asymptomatic CM1 in several morphometric MRI measures, including tentorium length, supraoccipital length, clivus-tentorium distance, clivus-torcula distance, total and caudal PCF volume, fourth ventricle volume to caudal PCF volume ratio, and CSF volume to caudal PCF volume ratio [ 103 ]. Later it was shown that 2D PCF morphometric measures did not correlate with the Chiari Severity Index [ 72 ]. Another study showed that symptomatic CM1 and CM0 patients had shorter clivuses than asymptomatic adults with low-lying tonsils [ 77 ]. One study showed that CM1 severity was more strongly associated with the descent of the obex than the tonsils. The authors suggested that the obex position was a better marker of clinical disease in CM1 patients and heterogeneous CM1 patient populations [ 101 ]. In another study, the fourth ventricle roof angle (FVRA) characterizing fourth ventricle bowing (a configuration change due to fourth ventricle outlet obstruction) was significantly greater in symptomatic CM1 patients than in asymptomatic CM1 and healthy controls. No difference was seen between asymptomatic CM1 patients and controls. The authors found that FVRA >65° predicted brainstem dysfunction better than tonsillar herniation >10 mm [ 104 ]. A clival canal angle <140° and obex below the FM were not associated with brainstem dysfunction in this study [ 104 ].
 Fuell and colleagues calculated the soft tissue density within the FM by dividing the soft tissue occupancy of the FM (cerebellar tonsils and brainstem) by the total area of the bony FM as a measure of impaction of the cerebellar tonsils in surgically treated symptomatic CM1 patients and conservatively managed asymptomatic CM1 patients [ 74 ]. The average tissue density was significantly greater in the surgical group than in the asymptomatic group—the soft tissue density, not the amount of TH, correlated with surgical intervention. The authors suggested that impaction of the cerebellar tonsils within the FM seen on MRI could identify some symptomatic patients that would respond well to surgical treatment, even though TH was less than the 5 mm traditionally needed for CM1 diagnosis. These patients had CM1-borderline and CM0.
 In CM1 spectrum disorders, the appearance and severity of symptoms depend on the crowdedness and compression of the hindbrain and CSF spaces within the FM, which is not adequately assessed on midsagittal MRI images [ 10 , 27 , 74 , 95 ]. Intraforaminal crowdedness and scarring play a more direct role in CM1 and CM0 pathogenesis and symptomatology than tonsillar descent [ 10 , 11 ]. Symptomatic CM1 and CM0 patients have statistically more significant disruption of CSF flow, including decreased flow posterior to the spinal cord and flow jets in the FM [ 80 , 105 ]. CSF flow imaging evaluates CSF flow pathways in symptomatic patients and predicts the response to decompressive surgery.
 6. The Crowded and Small PCF with CM1-like Clinical Manifestations and Syringomyelia (CM0-Syringomyelia)
 Iskandar and colleagues coined the term “Chiari Zero Malformation” to describe patients with cervical syringomyelia and <5 mm of TH ( Fig. 1 ) [ 14 ]. Since then, CM0 has been in the CM spectrum. CM0 patients have syringomyelia, little to no TH (<3 mm), and syrinx resolution after craniocervical decompression [ 15 , 20 , 21 ]. CM0 patients have a caudally displaced medulla on MRI and an arachnoid veil occluding the foramen of Magendie at surgery [ 15 , 36 ]. Patients with CM0 have SPCF and narrow CSF spaces like patients with classical CM1 [ 12 , 13 ]. Kyoshima identified a “tight cisterna magna” in CM0 patients and suggested its pathogenic link to syringomyelia [ 28 ]. Recently, CM0 was defined as “obliteration of the cisterna magna (due to arachnoid adhesions) and/or volumetrically small posterior fossa, with cerebellar tonsils positioned at the foramen magnum and syringomyelia in the cervical spinal cord” [ 30 ]. This definition of CM0 also requires associated syringomyelia ( Fig. 1 ) [ 21 , 30 , 40 ]. Many authors have commented on the pathophysiological similarities between CM0 and CM1 [ 12 , 13 , 18 , 19 , 27 , 37 ]. In CM0 patients, syrinx formation is associated with obstruction of CSF circulation by a small or distorted PCF, arachnoidal adhesions, or veils covering the foramen of Magendie [ 12 , 14 , 28 , 36 , 80 ].
 The pathophysiology of syringomyelia produced by CM1 is still controversial [ 9 , 18 , 90 , 100 , 106 ]. Most investigators agree that syringomyelia is related to abnormal CSF hydrodynamics at the FM. It is theorized that pulsatile movement of the cerebellar tonsils during the cardiac cycle drives CSF into the spinal subarachnoid space, through the spinal cord perivascular spaces, and into cord parenchyma [ 107 ]. In CM1 patients, syringes have been reported to occur more often in patients with more significant tonsillar herniation and CSF flow obstruction in many [ 31 , 58 ] but not all studies [ 108 ]. Taylor reported that CM1 patients with syringomyelia had reduced CSF space, shown by the FM axial MRI slice, and that surgery enlarging the CSF space was associated with syringomyelia resolution following surgery [ 95 ]. In CM1, the presence of syringomyelia correlated better with the narrowing of the FM subarachnoid CSF spaces on axial MRI than with the amount of TH seen on midsagittal MRI.
 Various factors have been associated with syringomyelia development in CM0 and CM1 patients: (a) reduced volume of the entire PCF [ 27 , 85 ] or area surrounding the FM [ 23 , 27 ]; (b) flattened PCF and decreasing clivus gradient (McRae line to clivus angle) [ 27 , 86 ]; (c) reduced anterior-posterior length of the PCF [ 109 ]; (d) cervical spinal canal tapering [ 27 , 110 ]; (e) caudal medulla shown by a caudal obex position [ 101 ]; (f) diminished CSF spaces at the FM [ 95 ]; and (g) obstructed CSF flow at the FM by tonsillar obstruction confirmed by cine MRI [ 106 ]. Syringes develop by similar mechanisms in CM1 and CM0 [ 12 , 36 , 80 ].
 We conducted a morphometric study of adult symptomatic patients with CM1 and CM0 [ 27 ]. Only patients with SPCF radiological traits were included in the study (the clivus length and supraocciput length in all patients were ≤40 mm, one standard deviation below the control group [ 2 ]). Patients were divided by MRI findings into four groups of 30 patients each: (1) SPCF with TH < 2 mm and without syringomyelia (SPCF-TH0-only); (2) SPCF with TH < 2 mm and with syringomyelia (SPCF-TH0-syr); (3) SPCF with TH ≥ 5 mm without syringomyelia (SPCF-CM1-only), and (4) SPCF with TH ≥ 5 mm with syringomyelia (SPCF-CM1-syr). We compared the four patient groups and an age and sex-matched control group. Comparison of the SPCF-TH0-syr group to the SPCF-TH0-only group showed that the SPCF-TH0-syr group had a significantly shorter anteroposterior diameter of the cervical canal at the first cervical vertebral (C1) level, more significant tapering of the cervical spinal canal, and greater PCF crowdedness, suggesting that upper cervical spinal canal narrowing and PCF crowding predisposed to syringomyelia in CM0 patients [ 27 , 95 ]. In patients with SPCF-TH0, narrowing of the inferior PCF, the FM, and the upper cervical canal was associated with syringomyelia [ 27 ]. Nishikawa reported similar findings in patients with CM1 symptoms, SPCF, and the absence of significant TH [ 23 ].
 Arachnoidal scarring in and around the FM may play a role in CM1 and CM0 symptomatology development and syrinx formation [ 14 , 36 , 111 – 113 ]. Arachnoidopathy in CM1 and CM0 may be a primary or secondary phenomenon. FM arachnoiditis or tonsillar herniation can obstruct CSF flow through the cisterna magna. About half of operated CM0 and syringomyelia patients had arachnoid veils occluding the fourth ventricular outlets or arachnoid adhesions in the cisterna magna [ 14 , 36 ]. CSF obstruction explains why syringomyelia is significantly more likely in CM1 cases with arachnoid changes than without [ 111 ]. In adult patients without TH but with syringomyelia consistent with CM0, a tight cisterna magna, intraoperatively verified obstruction of the foramen of Magendie, and syrinx resolution after craniocervical decompression were described [ 28 ].
 Despite some controversy about the pathogenesis of syringomyelia and other consequences of CM1 and CM0, there is agreement about compression of the medulla, cerebellar tonsils, and CSF flow pathways by osseous FM narrowing, crowding within the CCJ, or herniation of hindbrain structures into the spinal canal. Arachnoidal scarring can further alter CSF flow at the CCJ.
 The argument that CM0 can only be diagnosed if syringomyelia is present was founded on these premises: (1) Chiari-like symptoms are subjective, whereas a syrinx is objective; (2) there is no diagnostic test to differentiate non-syrinx CM0-symptoms from symptoms from other causes; and (3) the CM0 diagnosis may be confirmed only by surgical verification of the crowdedness or adhesions within the FM, postoperative alleviation of CM1-like symptoms, and syrinx collapse. However, this argument excludes patients seen in clinical practice who report CM1 symptoms, have MRI-evidence of crowdedness inside the FM funnel, but do not have associated syringomyelia. There is increasing evidence supporting the entity “CM0 without syringomyelia,” consisting of clinical signs and symptoms of “CM syndrome” and axial MRI and surgical findings of critical crowdedness inside the FM [ 13 , 27 , 29 , 37 ]. These and other methods of diagnosing CM0 without syringomyelia need further validation.
 7. The Crowded and Small PCF with CM1-like Clinical Manifestations Without Syringomyelia (CM0-Only)
 TH of less than 3 mm associated with CM1-like clinical manifestations traditionally is not enough to diagnose CM0 in the absence of syringomyelia. In the literature, this condition is named “spectrum of CM1,” “variant CM1,” “symptomatic tonsillar ectopia,” and “foramen magnum syndrome” [ 10 , 20 ] or in cases with confirmed SPCF as “Chiari-like syndrome” or “CM0-only” [ 13 , 27 ]. Like CM1, patients with SPCF and crowdedness inside the FM but without significant TH can be symptomatic, oligosymptomatic, or asymptomatic and may or may not have syringomyelia ( Fig. 1 ). Recently, in one center, more than 90 surgically confirmed cases of small PCF with CM1-like clinical manifestations without syringomyelia were described [ 29 ]. Clinical and radiological features of CM0 in patients with CM1-like clinical manifestations without syringomyelia were characterized in several other studies [ 11 , 13 , 18 , 23 , 27 , 37 , 72 ].
 In a study of 137 CM1 symptomatic patients and 14 CM0 symptomatic patients (36% without syringomyelia), both the CM1 and CM0 patient groups had an abnormally short clivus, reduced sagittal PCF area, and a narrowed (more acute) tentorium occipital bone angle. The clivo-axial angle was significantly wider in the CM0 group compared to the CM1 group, but other PCF morphometric measurements were similar between CM0 and CM1 [ 37 ]. In another study of 7 symptomatic CM0 patients (4 without syringomyelia) and 141 symptomatic CM1 patients (half of them without syringomyelia), the CM0 patient group had a longer clivus and supraoccipital bone than the CM1 group but smaller PCF cross-sectional area and a shorter clivus than the control group [ 11 ]. Defining factors of CM0 are (1) cerebellar tonsils found within but not below the FM, (2) brainstem caudal displacement and compression, (3) cerebellar tonsils obstructing CSF flow, and (4) membranes, adhesions, and scarring around the FM [ 29 , 36 , 80 , 114 ]. Heffez et al. studied a series of patients with Chiari-like symptoms and divided them into three groups by their amount of TH: (1) from 0 to less than 3 mm, (2) 3–5 mm, and (3) >5 mm. Only 4% of patients in Group 1 and 7.7% in the other two groups had syrinxes. These authors noted neural tissue compression within the FM in all groups of patients [ 29 ]. Interestingly, Group 1 patients had cerebellar tonsils within the FM compressing the brain stem and causing more severe symptoms than Groups 2 and 3. The presence of neurologic signs was not related to TH extent (mm) by logistic regression analysis [ 10 ]. All groups received the same CM1-type surgical decompression procedure. The surgeon noticed no difference between groups in intraoperative anatomy and physiology, deformity of the brain stem, neuroma of the C1 root, neural compression, posterior inferior cerebellar artery compression, or change in brain stem auditory evoked potentials. Surgical decompression helped all groups regardless of the amount of TH [ 29 ]. The authors concluded that typical CM1 signs and symptoms are more critical than TH extent, in mm, in diagnosing CM1 spectrum disorders and predicting surgical outcomes.
 In our study, patients with a small and overcrowded PCF without TH (SPCF-TH0) had a caudal hindbrain position like patients with CM1 (SPCF-CM1) [ 27 ]. In SPCF-TH0 patients, occipital bone hypoplasia, CSF pathway narrowing, and neural tissue crowding occurred just above and within the FM and upper cervical spine. Compared to the control, the SPCF-TH0 without syringomyelia group had reduced FM area and constriction of the ventral CSF space. The SPCF-TH0 without syringomyelia group but not the SPCF-CM1 without syringomyelia group had a narrow ventral CSF pathway, reduced FM area, and reduced anteroposterior diameter of the spinal canal at the C1 level [ 27 ]. Isolated atlas hypoplasia may cause tapering of the caudal continuation of the FM funnel [ 115 ]. In SPCF-CM1, occipital hypoplasia reduced the entire PCF volume. In CM1 and CM0-only (SPCF-TH0) patients, the PCF was abnormally small, and the hindbrain was abnormally caudally positioned. In SPCF-TH0, boney hypoplasia and neural tissue crowding occurred just above the FM and within the FM and upper cervical spine. The SPCF-CM1 without syringomyelia group had more neural crowding in the superior part of the PCF than the SPCF-TH0 group [ 27 ]. Nishikawa’s characterization of the SPCF traits in patients with CM1 and TH < 5 mm was consistent with our results [ 23 ].
 Patients with symptomatic CM1 without syringomyelia are surgically treated or conservatively managed if symptoms are mild. SPCF may produce symptoms of hindbrain compression and not syringomyelia. The term CM0 emphasizes the neural crowding and CSF obstruction within the FM region, not the amount of TH [ 15 ]. Heffez and colleagues reported their favorable results with surgical treatment of patients with FM crowdedness and TH ranging from 0 to <3 mm below the FM. Patients were preoperatively examined with axial FM MRI [ 29 ]. Recently, two other studies described a variant of cerebellar TH with ventrolateral TH but not the more typical posterior TH. This variant was coined “spectrum of CM1” or “CM 0.5” [ 20 , 22 ]. Axial imaging showed the lateral placement of the cerebellar tonsils at the cervicomedullary junction, which was outside the plane and unrecorded on the midsagittal MRI section [ 20 , 22 , 114 ]. Currently, much evidence supports using axial radiological imaging of the FM level to verify crowding in and around the FM in symptomatic patients without significant TH. Further investigation will lead to standard methods and metrics to diagnose classical symptomatic CM1 subtypes associated with SPCF and PCF crowdedness traits in and around the FM, like CM1-borderline, CM0.5, and CM0 without syringomyelia.
 Occasionally patients with crowded and latent small PCF without TH on midsagittal images may be admitted to a headache clinic with new daily persistent headaches, primary cough headache [ 116 ], or idiopathic intracranial hypertension, or to an orthopedic clinic with scoliosis [ 117 ]. Latent SPCF can also predispose to acute neurological deterioration following a single diagnostic lumbar puncture [ 118 ]. Prompt diagnosis of symptomatic, crowded, or small PCF is essential in these scenarios.
 8. Discussion
 CM1 with an isolated SPCF, called classical (primary) CM1, is the most common subtype of symptomatic CM1 [ 6 , 57 ]. Vega and colleagues reported that 76% of patients in their cohort of symptomatic CM1 patients had reduced PCF volume, area, linear measurements, and angular abnormalities at the skull base, causing PCF flattening [ 57 ]. Milhorat and colleagues, in a study including 741 patients with CM1, found that in 52% of these patients, PCF size and volume were significantly smaller than in the control group [ 6 ]. The other 48% of patients with TH had acquired CM1 with normal occipital bone size and PCF volume; in these patients, TH developed secondary to occipitoatlantoaxial joint instability from hereditary disorders of connective tissue or craniocervical trauma (34%), tethered cord syndrome (7%), intracranial mass lesions (4%), or lumboperitoneal shunts (3%) [ 6 ]. PCF radiological measurements defining SPCF were crucial for differentiating classical CM1 from acquired TH. In the classical type of CM1, TH is associated with PCF crowdedness.
 Classical CM1 spectrum disorders are heterogeneous groups with phenotypic variations. The morphological variability of SPCF derives from diverse environmental, genetic, and epigenetic etiologic factors. In families with CM inheritance, the SPCF trait is more consistently inherited than TH [ 43 , 44 ]. Genetics more strongly favor PCF morphologic features than TH, which has more external influences [ 43 , 44 ]. CM0 and CM1 subtypes coexist in these families by virtue of the multigenerational inheritance of SPCF morphometric features [ 19 ]. In our study of seven families with multiple members affected by CM1, 46 subjects had MRI-morphometric findings of SPCF, 17 of which had CM1 (TH ≥ 5 mm) with or without syringomyelia, 9 had borderline CM1 (TH 3–5 mm) with or without syringomyelia, and 20 had CM0 (TH < 3 mm) with or without syringomyelia [ 46 ]. CM1 and CM0 patients shared the SPCF phenotype, which was inherited. Epigenetic and environmental factors [ 19 , 43 ] can affect PCF morphology in CM1 entities and influence the development of CM0 or CM1.
 Clinicians should recognize variations of PCF architecture in CM1 patients [ 88 , 119 ]. Batzdorf adjusted his surgical approach according to the PCF morphology of his CM1 patients [ 119 ]. The recently proposed Nishikawa classification clarifies the different subtypes of classical CM1, including the subtypes with a normal PCF volume and isolated diminished volume around the FM [ 23 ]. His group reported impressive results from tailoring surgical approaches to specific types of CM1 [ 17 , 68 , 69 ]. Nishikawa’s patients with CM-1 Type A had a caudally positioned hindbrain but no reduction in the volume of the posterior cranial fossa (VPCF) or the volume around the FM (VAFM) and a normal occipital bone. The Type A patients’ treatments addressed conditions underlying hindbrain ptosis like craniocervical instability, tethered cord syndrome, and hydrocephalus. Patients with CM-1 Type B and CM-borderline had a normal VPCF, small VAFM, a small occipital bone, and crowded neural structures and CSF pathways around the foramen magnum. These CM subtype patients received foramen magnum decompression, preserving the arachnoid membrane. This procedure included a 2–3 cm square craniectomy around the foramen magnum, a C1 laminectomy, and a duraplasty. Nishikawa’s patients with CM-1 Type C had small volumes of the posterior cranial fossa (VPCF) and VAFM and a small occipital bone. The CM-1 Type C subtype patients received expansive suboccipital cranioplasty to expand the volume of the entire VPCF and the VAFM. This procedure included a craniectomy extending to the transverse and sigmoid sinuses, C1 laminectomy, arachnoid membrane preservation, duraplasty, and osteoplasty [ 17 ].
 In our clinical and MRI-morphometric study of symptomatic CM1 and CM0 patients without syringomyelia compared to normal controls, patients had a small, volumetrically reduced, flattened PCF with a slightly caudal position of hindbrain structures, a diminished height of the superior cerebellar cistern, and significant crowdedness in the FM [ 27 ]. Comparing symptomatic CM0 patients without syringomyelia to symptomatic CM1 patients without syringomyelia, the CM0 group had: (a) slightly less reduction of PCF volume, more significant reduction of the FM surface area and the anteroposterior diameter of the spinal canal at C1; (b) less descent of the fastigium toward the FM; and (c) less prominent reduction of superior cerebellar cistern height and slightly larger cisterna magna volume [ 27 ]. The location of the hypoplasia, tightness, and neural crowding in the CM0 without syringomyelia patients was in the PCF just above and within the FM and in the cervical spinal canal. In contrast, the superior PCF was less crowded in the CM0 patients without syringomyelia than in the CM1 patients without syringomyelia. The results of our CM0 study are consistent with the CM1-borderline subtype of classical CM1 in the Nishikawa and colleagues’ new classification [ 23 ].
 There are no established or expert-accepted diagnostic criteria for a crowded and small PCF in symptomatic patients. On axial MRI, Heffez studied 94 patients with Chiari-like symptoms without syringomyelia, TH < 3 mm below the FM, and FM crowdedness. During craniocervical decompression surgery, he recognized chronic neural compression in these patients like in CM1 patients [ 10 , 29 ]. His patients responded well to surgical decompression. Axial MRI through the FM plane was essential to diagnose these CM0 without syringomyelia patients [ 22 , 27 , 74 , 95 , 114 ]. Experts recommend that patients with symptoms of CM1, including occipital headache, cough headache, or Valsalva-like precipitated headache, undergo a complete neurological examination for signs of CM1 and its subtypes and a cranial and cervical spine MRI examination [ 4 , 10 , 13 , 27 , 49 ]. The MRI studies detect tonsillar ectopia, SPCF crowdedness, narrowing of the FM funnel, occipitalization or hypoplasia of the atlas, retro-odontoid soft-tissue thickness, odontoid angulation, and proximity of the odontoid tip to the FM plane. Cervical flexion-extension films or CT may be needed if atlantooccipital or atlantoaxial joint instability is suspected,
 Evaluating and diagnosing CM1 spectrum disorders requires recognition of SPCF traits beyond TH. Narrowing in the FM funnel often explains how minor cerebellar tonsillar descent can create enough crowdedness in the FM lumen to evoke CM1-like clinical manifestations [ 10 , 23 , 27 , 74 ]. Recognition of the role of PCF crowdedness in some patients with headaches is vital for the practicing neurologist and neurosurgeon [ 116 , 120 ].
 9. Limitations
 Occult or explicit CCJ anomalies or instability can create symptoms and signs mimicking those of CM0 and CM1. These associated conditions in studies of CM1 spectrum entities could lead to bias and inaccurate assumptions about SPCF symptoms and signs. CM1 morphometric studies vary considerably in the proportion of patients with (1) classical CM1 with SPCF, (2) acquired CM1 and normal PCF volume, and (3) TH < 5 mm.
 10. Conclusion
 Cerebellar tonsillar and hindbrain caudal descent associated with occipital bone hypoplasia and SPCF volume characterizes the classical (primary) CM1 spectrum abnormalities recently classified by Nishikawa and colleagues as CM1B, CM1C, and CM1-absent (CM1 with TH < 5 mm) [ 23 , 69 ]. The earlier CM subtypes, CM-borderline and CM0, are considered under this new classification as the “CM1-absent” subtype characterized by focal occipital bone hypoplasia in the PCF around the FM. These CM subtypes and classical CM1 with TH > 5 mm below the FM can manifest with or without syringomyelia. CM-borderline, CM0, CM1.5, and probably CM0.5 represent CM1 spectrum subtypes belonging to the same continuum of abnormalities with varying expression of diminishment and flattening of the PCF, caudal position of the hindbrain, and narrowing of FM CSF spaces. PCF morphometrics reflect the extent of occipital bone hypoplasia and SPCF. Several PCF morphological osseous features promote the hindbrain’s caudal position, and others hinder this process. Crowding of the neural elements in the FM and TH reflects morphological adaptations of the hindbrain to the disproportion between the SPCF and the normal hindbrain. Caudal positioning of the obex, fastigium, and cerebellar tonsils is characteristic of CM1 spectrum abnormalities. The radiologic PCF morphometric alterations in CM1 spectrum abnormalities reflect congenital, developmental, and adaptive pathophysiological processes.
 The types and severity of the clinical manifestations in CM1 spectrum syndromes exceed that expected by the amount of TH, which is <5 mm. Patients with CM1 spectrum syndromes should be evaluated with morphometrics sensitive enough to detect occipital hypoplasia and its manifestations. This testing can show (a) caudal positioning and deformity of the hindbrain shown by reduced obex to FM distance and TH, (b) occipital bone hypoplasia and SPCF, and (c) the extent of crowdedness inside the FM. This testing can also detect (d) acquired CM1 associated with a normal PCFV. Diagnosis of CM1 and CM1 spectrum abnormalities requires an understanding of the clinical presentation, anatomical variations, and pathophysiology of the various CM1 subtypes. Knowledge of the conditions leading to or associated with CM1 and CM1 spectrum abnormalities prepares the neurosurgeon to plan curative treatments. For example, CM1 spectrum patients with FM crowdedness confirmed preoperatively by axial MRI can receive treatment relieving neural compression in and around the FM. Further studies in this field should standardize radiological evaluation and diagnostic criteria for symptomatic CM0 and CM-borderline without syringomyelia.
