## 2. "Real-world sizes"

### Ground rules for all options

- **Use only the two numbers already on the site:** 5 mm (the usual cutoff) and 12 mm (the model's value, the average in one study of 160 people). Don't add 25 mm (the top of that study's range). On its own it could frighten people and reads like a "worst case".
- **Always follow it with the approved sentence:** "How far the tonsils extend doesn't always match how someone feels." This is already in COPY.md, the Home measurement myth, from EVIDENCE row 7.
- **Show both numbers the same way:**
  - same color and weight;
  - no green-to-red scale;
  - no words like "only", "just", "small", "large", "mild", "severe" or "worse".
- **Don't call anything "actual size" on screen.** Phones and monitors show millimeters at different sizes, so a drawn ruler can't be trusted to be true size.

The arithmetic, so the audit can check it:
- 3 quarters = 5.25 mm; 7 quarters = 12.25 mm;
- a quarter's width ÷ 5 = 4.85 mm; ÷ 2 = 12.13 mm;
- 3 pennies = 4.56 mm; 8 pennies = 12.16 mm;
- 4 dimes = 5.4 mm; 9 dimes = 12.15 mm.

### Three options

**Option A: a stack of quarters** (recommended for the English site)
> For a sense of size: 5 mm is about the height of 3 stacked U.S. quarters, and 12 mm is about 7. How far the tonsils extend doesn't always match how someone feels.

- Why: it uses one everyday coin for both numbers, and people can try it at home.

**Option B: one quarter's width**
> A U.S. quarter is about 24 mm across. 5 mm is about a fifth of that, and 12 mm is about half. How far the tonsils extend doesn't always match how someone feels.

- Why: it needs only one coin. But fractions are harder to picture than a stack.

**Option C: centimeters, no object** (recommended for the Spanish site, and as a fallback)
> 5 mm is half a centimeter. 12 mm is a little over 1 centimeter. How far the tonsils extend doesn't always match how someone feels.

- Why: it needs no outside source (it's unit conversion) and works in every country. U.S. coins mean little to many Spanish-speaking visitors.

I didn't use pennies. The U.S. Mint stopped making pennies for circulation in late 2025 (widely reported; I couldn't open a source for this). I also didn't use pencils, credit cards or paper. I couldn't open an official size for any of them (the ISO card standard page is blocked), and pencil sizes aren't standardized.

## 3. "Why does coughing set it off?"

### What the opened sources say, in plain words

When you cough, the pressure in your chest jumps for a moment. That swells the veins around the spinal cord and pushes some spinal fluid up from the spine into the head. In people without Chiari, it flows straight back down.

In Chiari I, the low tonsils can get in the way of the fluid coming back down. For a few seconds the pressure in the head is higher than in the spine. That pushes the tonsils further down into the opening and slows the fluid flow there.

Researchers believe this crowding may make symptoms worse after a cough. They agree that the tonsils hinder fluid flow, but not on the exact mechanism. Small MRI studies have seen the flow slow after coughing in people with Chiari I, and not in people without it.

### (a) Draft text for the site

**Option A (three sentences, recommended)**

> 1. A cough briefly raises the pressure in your chest, which pushes some spinal fluid up from the spine into the head. Normally it flows straight back down.
> 2. In Chiari I, the low tonsils can get in the way as the fluid flows back. For a few seconds, they can be pushed further down into the opening, and the flow there slows.
> 3. Researchers think this may help explain why coughing can bring on the classic headache, but they don't fully agree on how it happens, and not everyone with Chiari gets these headaches.

**Option B (adds a fourth sentence about the scans)**

> 4. In small MRI studies, fluid flow through the opening slowed for a few seconds after coughing in people with Chiari I, but not in people without it.

I recommend leaving sentence 4 off for now. A 2025 study (not yet peer reviewed; see "Flags") reports the opposite, and sentence 3 already says researchers don't fully agree.

**Typical view, when someone presses Cough there**

> The fluid moves up into the head and straight back down.

### (b) What the animation should show

Show one cough cycle in the **Chiari I view and the Typical view only**.

**The control**
- A real `button` labeled **Cough**. Press once to play one cycle; no holding needed.
- Holding is hard for keyboard, switch and some touch users. A real cough is also brief: holding fits straining better than coughing.

**What happens in Chiari I, in order**
1. **Rest.** The usual heartbeat fluid pulse, as the Explorer shows now.
2. **During the cough (under a second).** A soft pressure band travels up the fluid space around the spinal cord, from below the neck toward the head. Fluid particles move up through the opening into the head.
3. **Just after (a few seconds).**
   - The tonsils move down a short way into the opening, smoothly.
   - The fluid space at the opening narrows.
   - Fluid moving back down through the opening slows: particles get slower and sparser, but keep moving.
4. **Easing back (a few seconds).** The tonsils glide back to their usual Chiari I position, and the normal heartbeat pulse resumes.

**In the Typical view:** steps 1, 2 and 4 only. The fluid goes up and straight back down, the tonsils don't move, and the flow doesn't slow.

**Calm mode and `prefers-reduced-motion`:** no animation. Show three still frames instead, with **Previous** and **Next** buttons:
1. "During the cough"
2. "Just after"
3. "A few seconds later"

**Screen readers:** announce each step in an `aria-live` region, using sentences 1 and 2 split by step. Proposed `ariaLabel` strings, which reuse only the wording above:
- During the cough: "A cough briefly raises the pressure in your chest, which pushes some spinal fluid up from the spine into the head."
- Just after (Chiari I): "The low tonsils can get in the way as the fluid flows back. For a few seconds, they can be pushed further down into the opening, and the flow there slows."
- Just after (Typical): "The fluid moves up into the head and straight back down."
- A few seconds later: "Things ease back to how they were."

  This last line describes the animation, not a medical claim. The audit should confirm that, or replace it with "lasting only a few seconds before returning to the resting levels" in plain form.

### What the animation must NOT show

- **No distances or numbers.** No source I opened gives how far the tonsils move during a cough.
  - The closest figures are for the heartbeat, not coughing. With each heartbeat, the tonsils move well under 1 mm (Lawrence et al. 2018, [PMC6063776](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6063776/), opened, Table 5, summarizing other studies: "Leung et al., 2016 ... Tonsil ... 0.30 ± 0.10 mm" in healthy people vs "0.79 ± 0.45 mm" in Chiari I; "Cousins et al., 2009 ... 0.43 ± 0.06 mm" vs "0.57 ± 0.04 mm").
  - Don't quote these heartbeat figures for the cough, and don't put any number on the screen.
- **The measure readout must not change.** Hide or freeze the measure line while the cough plays. Otherwise the number would jump to a value no source supports, and the 12 mm (EVIDENCE row 15) would look like it changes with every cough.
- **No pain signs.** No lightning bolts, red flashes, warning colors, shaking or sound effects.
- **Flow is never stopped.** The sources say flow is "transiently" reduced, not blocked.
- **Only the fluid and the tonsils move.** The brainstem, spinal cord, skull, bones and syrinx stay still and keep their size. Nothing gets damaged.
- **The tonsils must return fully** to their usual Chiari I position, so the animation doesn't suggest each cough leaves them permanently lower.
- **Not in the syrinx or After decompression views.** I opened no source on what a cough does in those cases.
- **Nothing that reads as advice.** For example, nothing about avoiding coughing, lifting or straining. One opened review mentions this advice, but it is medical advice and is left out on purpose.
- **Never "every person" or "every cough".** The text keeps "can" and "not everyone".

### (c) Required caption

> Animation simplified. The size of the movement is drawn to be easy to see, not measured.

Keep the usual required captions too:

I didn't write "exaggerated", which you suggested. With no published value for how far the tonsils move during a cough, we can't say whether our drawing is bigger or smaller than real life.

The caption describes the drawing, not the body, but the audit should still check it.

## Flags: what I couldn't support or open

1. **A newer study disagrees with the cough explanation.**
   - "Chiari malformation does not obstruct cerebrospinal fluid flow during coughs" is a 2025 medRxiv preprint, not yet peer reviewed.
   - I saw it in search results only; medrxiv.org is blocked here.
   - Search summary: "In patients with Chiari, it is hypothesised that CSF flows freely into the cranium during a cough, but the cerebellar tonsils obstruct the caudal return of fluid post cough". The title says the authors' data did not show that obstruction.
   - This is why sentence 3 says researchers "don't fully agree" and why I recommend leaving out sentence 4. The independent auditor should try to open it.
2. **The second explanation isn't in the text.** Sansur et al. 2003 proposed that coughing over-pressurizes the spinal fluid and stretches the spinal covering. I know it only as summarized in Bhadelia 2011, which I opened ("attributed CAH ... to an exaggerated spinal CSF pressure response to coughing, from entrapment of spinal CSF by tonsillar herniation, resulting in stretching of the spinal dura"). The site text names neither theory, only "they don't fully agree on how it happens".
3. **The original pressure studies weren't opened.** These are Williams 1976, 1980 and 1981 (*Brain*, *Arch Neurol*, *Acta Neurochir*). Everything above rests on how the opened 2011–2024 papers describe them.
4. **No NINDS, MedlinePlus or StatPearls source for the mechanism.**
   - NINDS returned 403.
   - StatPearls (NCBI Bookshelf) showed a reCAPTCHA bot check. I stopped and didn't try to get past it.
   - The MedlinePlus Chiari page (opened; saved copy) has no pronunciation and no cough mechanism.
   - The cough rows rest on peer-reviewed papers only.
5. **Sneezing.** The mechanism papers talk about coughing and Valsalva (straining). They don't mention sneezing, so the cough text says "coughing", not "coughing, sneezing or straining". The approved headache line (row 4) stays as it is.
6. **Pronunciations and coin sizes:** search results only (see parts 1 and 2).
7. **PubMed Central** showed a bot check twice during this session. I waited and tried again later rather than getting around it. All six saved copies are the real article pages.
