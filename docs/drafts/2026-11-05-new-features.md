> 1. A cough briefly raises the pressure in your chest, which pushes some spinal fluid up from the spine into the head. Normally it then flows back down.

> 3. Researchers don't all agree on how this works, or whether it's why coughing can bring on the classic headache. Not everyone with Chiari gets these headaches.

## 3. "Why does coughing set it off?"

### What the sources say, in plain words

Older pressure studies and two small MRI studies, all peer reviewed, describe this sequence:
- When you cough, the pressure in your chest jumps for a moment.
- That swells the veins around the spinal cord and pushes some spinal fluid up into the head.
- In people without Chiari, it flows straight back down.
- In Chiari I, the low tonsils can get in the way as it comes back. For a few seconds the pressure in the head is higher than in the spine, which pushes the tonsils further down into the opening and, in those studies, slowed the flow.

Researchers believe this may make symptoms worse after a cough. They agree that the tonsils hinder fluid flow, but not on the exact mechanism.

**A newer study disagrees on the flow.** A 2025 study by a different team, posted as a preprint (not yet peer reviewed), is titled "Chiari malformation does not obstruct cerebrospinal fluid flow during coughs". See "How the 2025 preprint bears on the wording" below.

### (a) Draft text for the site (reworded after the preprint)

> 1. A cough briefly raises the pressure in your chest, which pushes some spinal fluid up from the spine into the head. Normally it flows straight back down.
> 2. One explanation is that, in Chiari I, the low tonsils get in the way as the fluid flows back, so for a few seconds they are pushed further down into the opening.
> 3. Researchers are still studying how this works and whether it's why coughing can bring on the classic headache. Not everyone with Chiari gets these headaches.

**Typical view, when someone presses Cough there**

> The fluid moves up into the head and straight back down.

**Changes from the first draft:**
- Sentence 2 now starts "One explanation is that…".
- Sentence 2 no longer says "and the flow there slows".
- Sentence 3 now says researchers are "still studying" it.
- The optional sentence about MRI studies showing slower flow is dropped.

### How the 2025 preprint bears on the wording

**What I could and couldn't read**
- **I could not read the preprint itself.** www.medrxiv.org answered with a Cloudflare block ("Sorry, you have been blocked"), which I did not try to get around.
- **Everything I know about it comes from search results only:**
  - The preprint's framing: "In patients with Chiari, it is hypothesised that CSF flows freely into the cranium during a cough, but the cerebellar tonsils obstruct the caudal return of fluid post cough". It tests this with real-time MRI and cites Bhadelia 2016 as the finding it re-examines.
  - A conference abstract from what appears to be the same group (ISMRM 2025, [archive.ismrm.org/2025/4727](https://archive.ismrm.org/2025/4727.html), blocked here), as summarized by search: CSF flow rates were similar between groups; Chiari patients' CSF flow "was not reduced following coughs, but CSF velocities were increased".
  - A news summary from Neuroscience Research Australia ([imaging.neura.edu.au](https://imaging.neura.edu.au/new-insights-into-chiari-what-happens-during-a-cough/), blocked here), as summarized by search: the fluid never stopped flowing, even with severe tonsillar descent, and a narrowed space in front of the brainstem may matter more than the tonsils.

**What this means for each part of the text**
- **Sentence 1 (fluid moves up into the head during a cough):** not contradicted. The preprint's own framing says CSF "flows freely into the cranium during a cough". Peer-reviewed support: Bezuidenhout 2018, Vandenbulcke 2024. **Keep.**
- **"The flow there slows" (first draft):** directly contested. Bhadelia 2016 and Bezuidenhout 2018 (8 and 13 patients) found it; the preprint and abstract report it was not reduced. **Removed** from the text and from the animation.
- **Sentence 2 (tonsils pushed further down after a cough):** this comes from the pressure-dissociation explanation (Williams, as described by Bhadelia 2016 and Bezuidenhout 2018). From the search summaries I can't tell whether the preprint measured tonsil movement. **Kept, but now framed as "One explanation is…"**, so it reads as a hypothesis, not a fact.
- **Sentence 3:** "still studying how this works and whether it's why…" now covers the disagreement. "Not everyone…" is supported by Vandenbulcke 2024 ("about one-third of the patients") and EVIDENCE row 6.

**Are the hedges enough?**
- **Yes, for the reworded text.** It states nothing about flow after a cough, and it labels the tonsil movement as one explanation still being studied.
- **The first draft was not hedged enough.** "...and the flow there slows" stated, as a fact, the very point the preprint disputes.
- **Caveat:** this judgment rests on search summaries of the preprint, not the paper. The independent auditor should read it once medRxiv can be opened (by a person in a browser if needed). If the preprint also finds that the tonsils don't move down after a cough, sentence 2 and the tonsil motion in the animation should be reconsidered or dropped.

### (b) What the animation should show

Show one cough cycle in the **Chiari I view and the Typical view only**.

**The control**
- A real `button` labeled **Cough**. Press once to play one cycle; no holding needed.
- Holding is hard for keyboard, switch and some touch users. A real cough is also brief: holding fits straining better than coughing.

**What happens in Chiari I, in order**
1. **Rest.** The usual heartbeat fluid pulse, as the Explorer shows now.
2. **During the cough (under a second).** A soft pressure band travels up the fluid space around the spinal cord, from below the neck toward the head. Fluid particles move up through the opening into the head.
3. **Just after (a few seconds).**
   - The tonsils move down a short way into the opening, smoothly, then hold briefly.
   - **The fluid keeps moving at its usual pace.** Particles don't slow, thin out or stop, because whether flow drops after a cough is disputed (see above).
4. **Easing back (a few seconds).** The tonsils glide back to their usual Chiari I position, and the normal heartbeat pulse resumes.

**In the Typical view:** steps 1, 2 and 4 only. The fluid goes up and straight back down, and the tonsils don't move.

**Calm mode and `prefers-reduced-motion`:** no animation. Show three still frames instead, with **Previous** and **Next** buttons:
1. "During the cough"
2. "Just after"
3. "A few seconds later"

**Screen readers:** announce each step in an `aria-live` region, reusing only the approved wording:
- During the cough: "A cough briefly raises the pressure in your chest, which pushes some spinal fluid up from the spine into the head."
- Just after (Chiari I): "One explanation is that the low tonsils get in the way as the fluid flows back, so for a few seconds they are pushed further down into the opening."
- Just after (Typical): "The fluid moves up into the head and straight back down."
- A few seconds later: "Things ease back to how they were."

  This last line describes the animation, not a medical claim; the audit should confirm that.

### What the animation must NOT show

- **No distances or numbers.** No source I opened gives how far the tonsils move during a cough.
  - The closest figures are for the heartbeat, not coughing. With each heartbeat, the tonsils move well under 1 mm (Lawrence et al. 2018, [PMC6063776](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6063776/), opened, Table 5, summarizing other studies: "Leung et al., 2016 ... Tonsil ... 0.30 ± 0.10 mm" in healthy people vs "0.79 ± 0.45 mm" in Chiari I).
  - Don't quote these heartbeat figures for the cough, and don't put any number on the screen.
- **The measure readout must not change.** Hide or freeze the measure line while the cough plays. Otherwise the 12 mm (EVIDENCE row 15) would look like it changes with every cough.
- **No slowed, thinned or stopped fluid.** It is disputed, so the animation shouldn't show it.
- **No pain signs.** No lightning bolts, red flashes, warning colors, shaking or sound effects.
- **Only the fluid and the tonsils move.** The brainstem, spinal cord, skull, bones and syrinx stay still and keep their size. Nothing gets damaged.
